Powered Loudspeakers + DSP...

Mar 2020STM322 min readBy Thei Munten

On the bench is a small board of my own making. It is the first step towards a powered loudspeaker that does exactly what I want: DSP inside the box, no front-panel controls, no rack of outboard gear. The details came later.

How did I get there? Start with the problem.

Why powered speakers?

Most loudspeaker boxes contain separate drivers for the high, low and mid frequencies, so we need a way to split the signal between them. Usually that is done with passive components — inductors and capacitors — and I could write a post about that one day. But for more control, and for that precious phase/time alignment, you need DSP.

In pro-audio this traditionally led to 19-inch amp racks filled with amplifiers and some DSP (maybe one of those Soundwebs). Things get bulky fast. If you only need one box — well, mostly two for stereo — perhaps as a fill-in, a delay or just some soundscape thing, something compact would be nice. Hence the popularity of powered boxes.

The difficult part

Most powered loudspeakers on the market are not at the quality level we appreciate. Those plastic boxes serve a purpose, but not ours. So here is the design goal: a powered box with a built-in DSP and amplifier, and no user controls.

First, we checked the market for a ready-made plate-amp that would fit inside our loudspeakers. People into this know the brands; yup, I tested them all, and I will not state my opinion on any of them. :) The main problem is that they are either limited in DSP (no FIR) or suffer badly from unstable software due to feature-itis. So, phase one:

Phase one: build a DSP unit

There you go: a home-brew DSP with a SigmaDSP ADAU1701, programmed in SigmaStudio. Check the mix of through-hole audiophile components (WIMA, Panasonic) and SMD parts — man, soldering these manually… aaarggh.

Nothing really new here, hardware-wise. Everybody knows those MiniDSP boards, but this one is programmed with the free SigmaStudio software, so we can do things like FIR.

Problem solved?

Hmmm, not really. It turns out mixing analog and digital circuitry is difficult, and the on-board ADCs are not the most brilliant. I did some tests with S/PDIF and I²S inputs, which improved the sonic quality, but things get complicated fast.

We could use a different DSP with a dedicated AD/DA section, but you will quickly find that the choice of self-boot chips is limited. All the rest will need some MCU to boot and control them.

Why not use an MCU?

And hey, here's an idea: why not use an MCU — you know, like an Arduino — to do all the processing? There are a zillion boards out there, and they are all dead cheap.

One tiny problem: not that many people are doing audio stuff with them, and Google doesn't really help. So stay tuned to find out more…
